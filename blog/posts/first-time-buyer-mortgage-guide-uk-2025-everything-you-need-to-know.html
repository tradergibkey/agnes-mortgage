First-Time Buyer Mortgage Guide UK 2025: Everything You Need to Know

October 8, 2026 · First time buyers · Agnes Mortgage

Agnes Siry Licensed Mortgage Advisor October 8, 2026

Buying your first home in the UK in 2025 means navigating a mortgage market that looks nothing like it did three years ago — higher rates, tighter affordability rules, and a shelf-full of government schemes that are partially withdrawn, partially replaced, and often misunderstood. The good news: there are at least seven concrete strategies that can get a first-time buyer from "no mortgage" to "offer accepted", and knowing which one fits your situation is the difference between completing and being stuck on the sidelines.

Why This Guide, and Who It's For

This article is for anyone buying their first residential property in England, Scotland, Wales, or Northern Ireland in 2025 — whether you're a salaried employee, a self-employed professional, a recent graduate with a small deposit, or a high-earner who still can't quite crack the affordability calculation. Each section below covers one key mechanism of the first-time buyer mortgage process, with honest numbers and honest trade-offs. No filler.

The 7 Things Every First-Time Buyer Must Understand in 2025

1. Deposit Size — Why 10% Is the Real Floor, Not 5%

While 95% mortgages (5% deposit) still exist in 2025, the pricing gap between 95% and 90% loan-to-value (LTV) products is stark. At 90% LTV, rates are typically 0.4–0.7 percentage points lower, which on a £250,000 mortgage can save over £80/month. Unless your only realistic option is a 5% deposit, stretching to 10% changes the market you have access to significantly. Most mainstream lenders — Halifax, Nationwide, Santander — price their best first-time buyer deals at 85–90% LTV.

2. Affordability Stress-Testing — How Lenders Actually Calculate What You Can Borrow

The headline rule is that most lenders cap borrowing at 4.49–4.75× your gross annual income, but the reality is more nuanced. Lenders stress-test your mortgage at a rate typically 3% above their standard variable rate (SVR) to ensure you could still afford repayments if rates rose sharply. Committed outgoings — car finance, student loan deductions, credit card minimums — reduce what lenders will offer more than most first-time buyers realise. A borrower with a £45,000 salary and £400/month in existing debt commitments may find their maximum offer is £165,000, not the £200,000 the income multiple suggests.

3. Fixed vs Tracker Rates — Choosing the Right Product in a Shifting Rate Environment

In 2025, two-year fixed rates for first-time buyers sit broadly between 4.2% and 5.1% depending on LTV, while five-year fixes cluster around 4.0%–4.9%. Base rate cuts from the Bank of England have been gradual, and most economists expect rates to settle in the 3.5–4.0% range by 2026–27. For first-time buyers who value payment certainty while they adjust to homeownership costs, a two-year fix gives optionality to remortgage when rates improve; a five-year fix locks in stability at a slightly lower rate today. Tracker mortgages carry genuine risk if cuts stall, but some come with no early repayment charges — useful if you expect to sell or remortgage within two years.

4. Mortgage in Principle — Get One Before You Make an Offer

A mortgage in principle (MIP), also called an agreement in principle (AIP), is a lender's conditional indication of how much they'll lend, based on a soft or hard credit check. Estate agents treat buyers without one as unserious — in a competitive market, sellers actively prefer buyers who can demonstrate mortgage readiness. Getting an MIP takes 20–40 minutes online with most lenders, is valid for 60–90 days, and costs nothing. One important note: multiple hard-credit-check MIPs in a short period can dent your credit score, so use a broker who can identify the right lender first rather than applying speculatively to four lenders in a week.

5. Government Schemes in 2025 — What's Still Available

Help to Buy Equity Loan closed to new applications in England in March 2023. The Mortgage Guarantee Scheme, which underpins most 95% LTV products, runs until June 2025. First Homes — a scheme offering newly built properties at a 30–50% discount to first-time buyers and key workers — is still active in England, though availability depends on local planning conditions. The Lifetime ISA (LISA) remains the most accessible nationwide benefit: save up to £4,000/year, receive a 25% government bonus (up to £1,000/year), and use it toward a first home worth up to £450,000. If you haven't opened a LISA yet and you're under 40, do it now — even a small contribution locks in your eligibility.

6. Joint Mortgages and Guarantor Products — Boosting Affordability Legitimately

Buying with a partner or friend increases the income multiple available because most lenders use a combined income figure. Some lenders apply 4× the higher income plus 1× the lower, while others use a flat 4.49× of combined income — the difference matters and varies by lender. If buying alone, guarantor mortgages (where a parent's income or property equity is used to supplement affordability) are still available through lenders including Barclays (Family Springboard) and Aldermore. These products typically require no additional deposit from the guarantor but do tie their credit to yours for the initial fixed period.

7. Complex Income as a First-Time Buyer — Self-Employed, Contractor, or Director

First-time buyers with non-standard income face an additional hurdle: lenders treating their income conservatively. A self-employed person with one year of accounts, a contractor paid via a limited company, or a salaried employee with significant bonus income will find that high-street banks often decline or significantly undervalue their affordability. Mortgages for directors and the self-employed are assessed very differently across lenders — some use net profit only, others use salary plus dividends, and a small group of specialist lenders use the most recent year's figures rather than a two-year average. Getting this wrong at the application stage doesn't just waste time; it leaves a hard credit search on your file. Agnes Mortgage's core specialism includes exactly this kind of case.

How to Pick the Right Approach for Your Situation

The right mortgage strategy for a first-time buyer depends on four factors: deposit size, income type, purchase price, and timeline. A straightforward employed buyer with a 10% deposit buying under £500,000 has access to the widest range of products and should prioritise rate over everything else. A self-employed buyer with a smaller deposit needs a lender who assesses income favourably before worrying about rate. A buyer using a LISA needs to confirm the property price falls under the £450,000 cap before committing. And anyone using a government scheme should confirm the scheme is still active in their region before factoring it into their plans.

Key Takeaways

  • A 10% deposit unlocks significantly better rates than 5% — the monthly saving is material, not marginal.
  • Affordability is determined by stress-tested income minus committed outgoings, not just your salary multiplied by 4.5.
  • A mortgage in principle should be in place before you make any offer — it costs nothing and signals seriousness to sellers.
  • The Lifetime ISA is the single best government benefit still available to most first-time buyers in 2025 — open one if you haven't.
  • Non-standard income requires a lender match before a rate comparison — the wrong lender for a self-employed buyer can undervalue income by 20–40%.

Frequently asked questions

How much deposit do I need to buy my first home in the UK in 2025?

The minimum deposit for most first-time buyers is 5% of the purchase price, supported by 95% LTV mortgage products. However, a 10% deposit unlocks significantly better interest rates — typically 0.4–0.7% lower — which can reduce monthly payments by £80 or more on a £250,000 mortgage. Stretching to a 10% deposit is strongly worth considering if your timeline allows it.

How much can a first-time buyer borrow in the UK?

Most lenders apply an income multiple of 4.49–4.75× your gross annual salary, but they also stress-test at higher rates and deduct existing debt commitments like car finance, student loans, and credit card minimums. A borrower earning £45,000 with £400/month in existing commitments may be offered considerably less than the headline income multiple suggests. The only way to get an accurate figure is to run your profile through a broker or lender's affordability model.

Is Help to Buy still available for first-time buyers in 2025?

The Help to Buy Equity Loan scheme closed to new applicants in England in March 2023 and is no longer available. The Mortgage Guarantee Scheme, which supports 95% LTV lending, is running until June 2025. The Lifetime ISA remains one of the most accessible benefits — contributing up to £4,000/year earns a 25% government bonus, usable on a first home worth up to £450,000.

Can I get a mortgage as a self-employed first-time buyer in the UK?

Yes, but lender criteria vary significantly. Some require two years of accounts and use a two-year average net profit; others accept one year of accounts or assess salary plus dividends for limited company directors. High-street lenders are often more restrictive, while specialist lenders assess income more favourably. Working with a broker who understands self-employed underwriting is essential to avoid applying to the wrong lender and leaving unnecessary hard credit searches on your file.

Should I get a fixed or tracker mortgage as a first-time buyer in 2025?

For most first-time buyers in 2025, a two-year or five-year fixed rate offers the most sensible balance of rate certainty and flexibility. Two-year fixes allow you to remortgage sooner if the Bank of England cuts rates further, while five-year fixes provide stability at a marginally lower rate today. Tracker mortgages are riskier if rate cuts slow, though some carry no early repayment charges — useful if you plan to move or remortgage within two years.
