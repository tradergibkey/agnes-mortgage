What Credit Score Do You Need to Buy a House in the UK?

October 14, 2026 · Agnes Mortgage

Agnes Siry Mortgage Broker October 14, 2026

There is no single credit score that unlocks a UK mortgage — and the idea that you need a "good" score before you can even apply is one of the most persistent myths in property finance. Your credit score matters, but it is one signal among many, and lenders read it very differently from how the consumer agencies present it to you.

Credit Scores Are Lender-Specific — Not a Universal Pass Mark

Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion each produce their own score on their own scale. Experian runs to 999, Equifax to 1,000, TransUnion to 710. None of these numbers appears on a lender's underwriting screen. What lenders actually see is the raw data behind the score — your payment history, credit utilisation, electoral roll registration, court judgments, defaults, and account age — and they apply their own proprietary scoring model to it.

That means a score of 650 on Experian might be classed as "fair" by the agency but rated quite differently by Halifax, Nationwide, or a specialist lender. The agency score is a consumer-friendly shorthand. It is useful for spotting problems, but it is not the number that gets your mortgage approved.

What lenders care about underneath the score:

  • Payment history: missed or late payments in the last 24 months carry the most weight
  • Defaults and CCJs: a registered default can stay on your file for six years; a satisfied default is viewed more charitably than an unsatisfied one
  • Electoral roll registration: not being registered is a surprisingly common reason for automated declines
  • Credit utilisation: using more than 30–40% of your available revolving credit consistently signals stress to most models
  • Account age and mix: a thin file with few accounts can score poorly even with no adverse history

Most High-Street Lenders Want a Clean File for the Last Two Years

For mainstream lenders — think Halifax, Barclays, HSBC, Santander — the practical requirement is a file with no missed payments, no defaults, and no CCJs in the preceding 24 months. Your Experian score in this scenario will typically sit above 700, and Equifax above 600, but the clean conduct record is what actually drives the approval, not the headline number.

First-time buyers with limited credit history often find they score poorly simply because the file is thin. Agencies penalise novelty. Adding a credit card, using it lightly, and paying it in full each month for six to twelve months before applying can add 40–80 points to a consumer score — not because debt is good, but because visible, well-managed credit gives lenders something to assess.

The deposit size also interacts with creditworthiness in a way most buyers underestimate. At 85% loan-to-value (LTV), lenders apply tighter automated criteria than at 75% LTV. If your credit file has minor blemishes — a late payment two years ago, a small settled default — moving from a 15% to a 25% deposit can shift you from a decline to an approval at the same lender. Skin in the game reduces lender risk, and lenders price that reduction into their willingness to accept marginal credit profiles.

Adverse Credit Does Not Mean No Mortgage — It Means a Different Lender

Here is where many buyers either give up unnecessarily or make costly mistakes by applying to the wrong lenders. A history of defaults, CCJs, a satisfied debt management plan, or even a previous bankruptcy does not close the mortgage market to you. It redirects you toward specialist and near-prime lenders — Kensington, Pepper Money, Bluestone, Together, and others — who lend explicitly into adverse credit profiles, at a higher interest rate, usually requiring a larger deposit (typically 15–25%).

The key variables in adverse credit lending are:

  • Recency: a default registered four years ago is treated very differently from one registered four months ago
  • Severity: a single missed phone bill is not comparable to a CCJ for £10,000
  • Satisfaction: a default marked "satisfied" is significantly better than one still outstanding
  • Total adverse value: lenders often have maximum thresholds — £2,500 total adverse in the last 36 months, for example

Knowing exactly where your adverse entries sit against these thresholds is what separates a well-placed application from a credit-scarring shotgun approach. Applications leave hard-search footprints, and multiple footprints in a short window further damage the score you are trying to protect.

For buyers in this position, residential mortgages through a specialist broker — rather than a direct bank application — are almost always the right route. A broker can match your specific adverse profile to the lender whose criteria it fits before a single hard search is made.

The Counter-Argument: Score-Building Takes Time You Might Not Have

The honest objection to "just improve your score first" is that property markets do not pause while you build credit history. A buyer who needs 12 months of positive credit conduct before qualifying may lose their target property, face a different rate environment, or simply miss the right point in their personal circumstances — a job move, a life event, a fixed window for buying near family.

This is a legitimate tension, and there is no clean answer. What there is, however, is precision. Rather than spending a year improving a score in a vacuum, the smarter move is to know, right now, which lenders would approve you in your current state — and at what rate and LTV. If the rate premium for applying now versus waiting 12 months is 0.8%, you can model whether that costs more over the fixed term than the opportunity cost of waiting. That is a decision, not a guess.

Specialist brokers can usually run this analysis without triggering hard searches. The information exists to make a rational choice; most buyers simply never access it.

Your Score Matters — But Not in the Way You Think

To restate the position, now with that nuance accounted for: your credit score as displayed by a consumer agency is a useful diagnostic tool, not an eligibility threshold. The underlying data — particularly payment conduct in the last two years, any county court judgments, and your electoral roll status — matters enormously. The three-digit number displayed on Experian does not.

Most buyers with a clean recent history and a 10% or greater deposit will find a mainstream lender. Buyers with adverse credit will find a specialist lender, at a cost, with the right advice. Buyers with thin files should invest six to twelve months in visible, well-managed credit before applying — but should also model what the wait costs them. The score is a map, not a gate. Read the terrain, then decide when to move.

Key Takeaways

  • No universal minimum credit score exists for a UK mortgage — lenders use their own models applied to raw credit data, not agency scores.
  • Most high-street lenders want a clean payment record for the last 24 months; the headline score matters less than what sits behind it.
  • Adverse credit — defaults, CCJs, DMPs — does not disqualify you; it redirects you to specialist lenders with different rate and deposit requirements.
  • A larger deposit can compensate for a weaker credit profile at many lenders, especially at or below 75% LTV.
  • Multiple direct applications leave hard-search footprints that worsen your position; using a broker to match criteria before applying is materially safer.

Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum credit score to get a mortgage in the UK?

There is no single minimum credit score — each lender applies its own model to raw credit data, not the consumer agency scores shown by Experian, Equifax, or TransUnion. What matters most is your payment conduct in the last 24 months, whether you have any CCJs or defaults, and whether you are on the electoral roll.

Can I get a mortgage with a bad credit score in the UK?

Yes, but you will likely be directed to specialist or near-prime lenders such as Kensington, Pepper Money, or Bluestone rather than high-street banks. These lenders will typically require a larger deposit — often 15–25% — and charge a higher interest rate that reflects the additional risk.

How long does it take to improve your credit score enough to get a mortgage?

For a thin file with no adverse history, six to twelve months of consistent, well-managed credit use can add 40–80 points to a consumer score and meaningfully improve lender eligibility. If you have recent defaults or CCJs, the wait is typically until those entries are at least two to three years old.

Does applying for a mortgage affect my credit score?

Yes — each full mortgage application triggers a hard credit search, which leaves a visible footprint on your file and can lower your score by a small amount. Multiple applications in a short period compound this effect. Using a whole-of-market broker to match you to the right lender before applying reduces the number of hard searches needed.

Does the size of my deposit affect what credit score I need for a mortgage?

In practice, yes. Lenders apply stricter automated criteria at higher loan-to-value ratios, so a buyer with a 25% deposit will generally be approved where the same buyer with a 10% deposit might not be. A larger deposit reduces lender risk and can offset a less-than-perfect credit profile at many lenders.
