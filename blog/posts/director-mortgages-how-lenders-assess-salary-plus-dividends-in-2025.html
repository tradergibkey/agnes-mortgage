Director Mortgages: How Lenders Assess Salary Plus Dividends in 2025

September 5, 2026 · Self-Employed & Director Mortgages · Agnes Mortgage

Agnes Siry Licensed Mortgage Advisor September 5, 2026

Getting a mortgage as a limited-company director is harder than it should be — but knowing exactly how each lender reads your income changes everything.

Most high-street banks are built around payslips. If your income arrives as a combination of salary and dividends — as it does for the vast majority of UK limited-company directors — standard affordability calculators either undercount what you earn or reject you outright. The good news: there are at least seven distinct lender approaches to director income in 2025, and matching yourself to the right one is the single most powerful lever you have.

This guide breaks down each approach, what it means for your borrowing capacity, and which type of director benefits most from each method. Whether you draw a modest salary with heavy dividends, retain profit inside your company, or have only one year of accounts, there is a viable path — provided you apply to the lender that suits your structure.

The 7 Ways Lenders Assess Director Income in 2025

1. Salary Plus Dividends (Last 2 Years' Average) — The Mainstream Method

The most widely used approach: the lender adds your personal salary to your personal dividend drawings and averages the two most recent tax years. This is how most high-street banks — Halifax, NatWest, Santander — calculate your income. If your drawings have been consistent and well-documented on SA302s and tax year overviews, this method works well. The snag: if Year 1 was a lean start-up year and Year 2 was strong, averaging drags your figure down significantly.

2. Latest Year Only — Better for Growing Businesses

A smaller but important group of specialist lenders will assess income from the most recent tax year only, without averaging. This is particularly valuable if your company has grown sharply — say, £45,000 drawn in Year 1 and £90,000 in Year 2. Under the latest-year-only approach, lenders use £90,000; under the two-year average, they use £67,500. That difference can equate to over £100,000 in borrowing capacity at typical income multiples. Lenders such as Kensington and certain private banks apply this logic for the right profile.

3. Salary Plus Net Profit (for Majority Shareholders) — The Director's Advantage

Some lenders — notably Halifax under their specialist criteria and several building societies — will add your personal salary to the company's net profit before tax, rather than just your dividend withdrawals. This is a genuine step-change for directors who retain profit inside the company rather than drawing it. If your salary is £12,570 and you retained £80,000 net profit in the company, an SA302 shows only £12,570 in personal income — but a lender using net profit sees £92,570. You must typically own 25% or more of the company to qualify for this approach.

4. Salary Plus Gross Profit Share — Sector-Specific Flexibility

A handful of lenders, including some specialist and private banks, will use a share of gross profit proportional to your ownership stake, then add salary on top. This method tends to benefit directors in high-turnover, lower-margin businesses — think contracting companies or certain trade sectors — where net profit understates the true economic picture. It requires clean, current accounts and an accountant's letter confirming your ownership percentage and the business trading position.

5. One Year of Accounts — For Newer Directors

Most lenders require two full years of self-employed accounts or SA302s. However, a growing number of specialist lenders will accept just one completed tax year, provided the business has a clear operating history and you can evidence the pipeline or sector stability. Lenders such as Precise Mortgages and Accord operate in this space. The trade-off is usually a slightly higher rate or a lower loan-to-value cap — typically 85% LTV rather than 90% — but for a director who incorporated recently, this can be the difference between buying now or waiting another 12 months.

6. Retained Profit Add-Back — For Profit-Rich, Low-Drawing Directors

Some directors deliberately minimise drawings to reduce personal tax, leaving healthy cash reserves inside the company. Standard income assessment penalises this severely. A small number of lenders — predominantly private banks and specialist whole-of-market lenders — will add back a proportion of retained profit to the assessed income figure. This is sometimes called an "add-back" approach and requires up-front negotiation, strong business accounts, and usually a minimum loan size of £500,000. It is niche, but for asset-rich, income-light directors, it is often the only route to the loan they need.

7. Income Multiples for Directors — How Far Lenders Will Go

Beyond how income is calculated, the multiple applied to that income varies materially by lender. Standard residential lenders typically cap at 4.5x income, in line with the FCA's responsible lending guidance and the PRA's limits for high-LTV lending. But several specialist and private lenders will go to 5x or 5.5x for professionals — including company directors with strong accounts and low personal debt. A director with assessed income of £100,000 sees their maximum borrowing shift from £450,000 to £550,000 purely by choosing a 5.5x lender. Stress-testing, loan size, and LTV all affect eligibility for enhanced multiples.

What to Look for When Choosing Your Approach

The right lender method depends on three variables: how your income is structured, how long your company has been trading, and whether you prioritise the highest loan size or the lowest rate. Here is how to think through each:

  • Consistent drawings for 2+ years? The mainstream salary-plus-dividends average works fine. Focus on rate and fees rather than criteria flexibility.
  • Growing income or only one year of accounts? Target lenders that use the latest year only, or those accepting one year of accounts. A specialist broker is essential here — these lenders rarely advertise their criteria clearly.
  • Retaining significant profit in the company? The net-profit or add-back approach is the priority. This almost always requires professional broker intervention, as standard sourcing systems won't surface these lenders.
  • Large loan (above £750,000)? Private banks become relevant. They assess income holistically — assets, company health, future earnings — and the process is relationship-driven rather than algorithmic.
  • Low salary, high dividends? Ensure your SA302s and tax year overviews are current before approaching any lender. Lenders assess personal income as declared to HMRC — company bank balances are irrelevant at most mainstream institutions.

For mortgages for directors and the self-employed, the lender panel matters as much as the rate. A whole-of-market broker with access to specialist lenders — not just the familiar high-street names — can often find a solution when a direct bank application has already failed.

If your income is growing, your drawings are modest relative to company profit, or you have fewer than two years of accounts, do not approach a high-street bank first — you will likely be declined on criteria that a specialist lender would not apply.

Key Takeaways

  • There are at least seven distinct methods lenders use to assess director income — choosing the right one can increase your borrowing capacity by £100,000 or more.
  • The two-year average of salary plus dividends is the mainstream approach, but it penalises directors with growing or retained income.
  • Net profit add-back and salary-plus-net-profit methods are available through specialist lenders for directors who retain profit inside the company.
  • Income multiples range from 4.5x at standard lenders to 5.5x at specialist and private banks — the difference on a £100,000 income is £100,000 in additional borrowing.
  • One-year-of-accounts options exist, but come with LTV restrictions; a whole-of-market broker is the most efficient way to find and qualify for them.

Frequently asked questions

How do mortgage lenders calculate income for limited company directors?

Most lenders add your personal salary to your dividend drawings and average the last two tax years, using figures from your SA302s. Some specialist lenders instead use salary plus net company profit, which benefits directors who retain earnings inside the business rather than drawing them personally.

Can I get a mortgage with only one year of accounts as a director?

Yes, a number of specialist lenders — including Precise Mortgages and Accord — will consider applications with just one completed tax year. You will typically face a lower maximum LTV (around 85%) and slightly higher rates, but it removes the requirement to wait for a second set of accounts.

What income multiple can a limited company director get on a mortgage?

Standard lenders apply a cap of 4.5x assessed income in line with FCA and PRA guidance. Specialist and private lenders can go to 5x or 5.5x for directors with strong accounts and low personal debt, which can translate to an additional £100,000 in borrowing on a £100,000 assessed income.

Do lenders count retained profit inside a limited company for mortgage purposes?

Most high-street lenders do not — they assess only income declared to HMRC on your SA302. However, a minority of specialist and private bank lenders offer a 'net profit add-back' approach that incorporates retained company profit into the affordability calculation, typically for larger loans above £500,000.

Why was my director mortgage application declined by my bank when my company is profitable?

High-street banks use personal tax declarations (SA302s) rather than company accounts or bank balances to assess income. If your declared personal income is low — because you retain profit in the company or draw mainly dividends — standard affordability models will undercount your real earnings. A specialist lender using net-profit-based assessment is likely more appropriate.
