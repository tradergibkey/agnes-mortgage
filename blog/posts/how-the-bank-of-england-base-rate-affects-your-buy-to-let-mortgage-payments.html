How the Bank of England Base Rate Affects Your Buy-to-Let Mortgage Payments

October 1, 2026 · UK Mortgage Market Insights & Rate Trends · Agnes Mortgage

Agnes Siry Licensed Mortgage Advisor October 1, 2026

The Situation: A Portfolio That Made Sense at 0.1% — and Didn't at 5.25%

Marcus held six buy-to-let properties across the East Midlands. He'd built the portfolio steadily between 2015 and 2021, each acquisition stress-tested against rental yields and mortgage payments at the rates that existed at the time. On paper, the numbers were clean. Combined rental income of roughly £5,800 per month, total mortgage payments of around £2,600, decent margins on every property.

Then, between December 2021 and August 2023, the Bank of England raised its base rate 14 consecutive times — from a historic low of 0.1% to 5.25%. Marcus had two properties on tracker mortgages. Four were on fixed rates, but those fixes were expiring between mid-2022 and early 2024. By the time the last fix rolled off and he remortgaged onto the prevailing market rate, his total mortgage payments had climbed to just over £4,700 per month. Same properties. Same tenants. Same rent. But £2,100 more leaving his account every month.

He wasn't unusual. Thousands of landlords across the UK faced the same arithmetic. But how Marcus responded — and what he understood too late about the mechanics driving his payments — is a lesson worth examining in detail.

The Mechanics: How Base Rate Actually Feeds Into Your Mortgage

The Bank of England base rate is the interest rate at which commercial banks borrow overnight from the Bank of England. It functions as the floor for the cost of money across the entire financial system. When it moves, mortgage rates follow — but not always immediately, and not always proportionally.

Tracker mortgages: direct exposure

A tracker mortgage is priced at a fixed margin above the base rate — for example, base rate + 1.5%. If base rate sits at 0.1%, you pay 1.6%. If base rate moves to 5.25%, you pay 6.75%. The change lands in your next monthly payment, often with just 30 days' notice. Marcus had two trackers. When base rate hit 4%, those two mortgages alone were costing him £780 more per month than in 2021.

Fixed-rate mortgages: deferred exposure

Fixed rates insulate you for the term of the deal — typically two or five years. But lenders price new fixed-rate products against swap rates, not the base rate directly. Swap rates reflect the market's expectation of where base rate will be over the fixed period. When the hiking cycle began in late 2021, swap rates rose sharply in anticipation of further increases. This meant that even landlords who hadn't yet come off a fix were being quoted significantly higher rates when they shopped around. By mid-2022, a two-year buy-to-let fix that would have been available at 1.9% in 2021 was being quoted at 3.8%. By late 2022, 5.5% to 6% was common.

Standard variable rates: the worst-case scenario

A lender's standard variable rate (SVR) is a discretionary rate set by the lender itself. It broadly tracks base rate but with no obligation to match it exactly. SVRs are typically 2–3 percentage points above the base rate, and they represent the default rate a borrower lands on when a fixed deal expires and they take no action. For buy-to-let properties, SVRs during 2023 were running at 8% to 9.5% at many lenders. Sitting on SVR on a £200,000 interest-only buy-to-let mortgage at 9% costs approximately £1,500 per month. At 2%, that same mortgage cost around £333.

Interest-only magnifies rate sensitivity

Most buy-to-let mortgages are structured on an interest-only basis. This means your monthly payment is entirely composed of interest — there is no capital repayment element to cushion the effect of rate movement. Every basis point of rate change translates directly into a change in your payment. On a £250,000 interest-only mortgage, a 1% rate increase costs you an additional £208 per month. A 4% increase over two years — which is approximately what happened between 2021 and 2023 — costs an additional £833 per month on that single loan alone.

What Marcus Tried — and Where the Strategy Went Wrong

When his first fix expired in mid-2022, Marcus remortgaged that property onto a new two-year fix. The rate was 3.9% — painful compared to his previous 1.85%, but still manageable. His logic was that rates would fall back within two years. Reasonable at the time.

For the two tracker properties, he held on. He expected the hiking cycle to peak and reverse quickly. It didn't. He was also under the impression that switching trackers mid-term would trigger early repayment charges. On one of the two products, that was correct. On the other, there were no ERCs — he could have switched at any point. He hadn't checked the product terms carefully enough.

By the time the remaining fixed-rate properties came off deal in late 2023, he was remortgaging into a market where buy-to-let rates had softened slightly from their 2023 peaks but were still sitting between 4.8% and 5.6%. He also encountered a problem he hadn't anticipated: the lender's rental stress test.

Most buy-to-let lenders require rental income to cover mortgage payments by a minimum ratio — typically 125% at a stressed rate, often calculated at the higher of the product rate plus 2% or a notional 5.5%. With rates now materially higher, several properties that had previously passed this test no longer did at the loan amounts Marcus wanted to maintain. One lender declined his application entirely. Another would only lend at a lower LTV, requiring him to partially repay the mortgage at renewal.

He hadn't planned for this. He had no liquid capital set aside to reduce the loan. It resulted in a four-month delay while he arranged the funds, during which the property sat on SVR at 8.74%.

The Resolution: What Changed, and What It Cost

Working with a whole-of-market broker, Marcus reviewed every property individually. The two trackers were addressed first. The one without ERCs was immediately switched to a five-year fix at 5.1% — reducing the payment uncertainty and locking in a rate before any further movement. The tracker with ERCs was assessed against the cost of breaking early versus waiting for the ERC window to close; the numbers favoured waiting six months, then fixing.

For the remaining properties, the broker sourced lenders whose stress tests were calculated at the product rate rather than a loaded notional rate — a meaningful difference when product rates sit below 5.5%. This allowed Marcus to maintain his existing loan balances on three of the four properties.

The net result: monthly mortgage costs stabilised at £3,950 — still £1,350 more than in 2021, reflecting the genuine repricing of the rate environment, but £750 less per month than the unmanaged position he'd drifted into. Over a two-year fixed term, that difference is £18,000. The broker fee for structuring the full portfolio review was £500 per case — a marginal cost against the saving achieved.

For landlords with complex income structures or multi-property portfolios, buy-to-let mortgages require lender-by-lender analysis, not a single remortgage comparison. Each property's LTV, rental yield, and the landlord's wider tax position interact with lender criteria in ways that a direct bank application will never surface.

The Lesson: Rate Sensitivity Is Structural, Not Just Cyclical

Marcus's experience is not primarily a story about the 2021–2023 rate cycle. It's a story about structural vulnerabilities in a buy-to-let portfolio that existed long before rates moved — he just didn't know they were there until they mattered.

Several specific lessons apply broadly:

  • Know your product terms before a crisis. ERC schedules, tracker floors, and reversion rates are contractual details that determine your options when rates move. Read them before you need them.
  • Model your portfolio at multiple rate scenarios. If your cash flow only works at sub-2% rates, your business model is fragile. Stress test at 4%, 5%, and 6% when you acquire, not when you're renewing.
  • Rental stress tests move with rates. The lender's affordability calculation at renewal may be materially different from the one used at origination. If rates have risen significantly, you may face a lower maximum loan — plan for that possibility with liquid reserves.
  • Tracker vs fixed is a risk allocation decision. Trackers benefit you when rates fall; fixes protect you when rates rise. The choice should reflect your risk tolerance and your view on the rate cycle — not just the headline rate at the time.
  • SVR is almost never the right answer. Drifting onto SVR — even for a few months — is typically the most expensive thing a buy-to-let investor can do. Set calendar reminders six months before every product expiry.

The base rate will continue to move in both directions across any portfolio's lifetime. What determines whether those moves damage or merely inconvenience a landlord is almost always how well the portfolio was structured and monitored before the change — not how quickly they reacted afterwards.

Key Takeaways

  • Tracker mortgages pass base rate changes directly to your monthly payment, often within 30 days; fixed rates defer the impact until renewal.
  • Interest-only buy-to-let mortgages are fully rate-sensitive: a 1% rate rise on a £250,000 loan costs approximately £208 more per month.
  • Lender stress tests tighten when rates rise, and can reduce the loan amount available at renewal — even on properties you already own.
  • SVR is typically 2–3 percentage points above base rate; sitting on it at a major lender in 2023 meant paying 8–9.5%, making it the most avoidable cost in a portfolio.
  • A full portfolio review with a whole-of-market broker — rather than lender-by-lender direct applications — is the most reliable way to navigate a rate environment like this one.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Bank of England base rate directly affect my buy-to-let mortgage?

It depends on your mortgage type. Tracker mortgages move in direct lockstep with the base rate, so a rate rise immediately increases your monthly payment. Fixed-rate mortgages are insulated until the deal expires, but new fixed rates are priced off swap rates, which anticipate future base rate movements and typically rise before the base rate does.

How much does a 1% rise in base rate add to a buy-to-let mortgage payment?

On an interest-only mortgage of £250,000, a 1% rate rise adds approximately £208 per month. On a £400,000 loan, the same increase adds around £333 per month. Because most buy-to-let mortgages are interest-only, there is no capital repayment to absorb rate changes — every basis point hits your payment directly.

What happens to my buy-to-let mortgage when my fixed rate expires?

When your fixed term ends, you automatically move to the lender's standard variable rate (SVR) unless you remortgage or take a new product. SVRs are typically 2–3 percentage points above the base rate and can run to 8–9% in a high-rate environment. You should begin the remortgage process at least six months before your deal expires to avoid landing on SVR.

Can a rising base rate stop me from remortgaging my buy-to-let?

Yes — indirectly. Lenders apply a rental stress test at renewal, requiring rent to cover mortgage payments at a stressed rate, typically 125% at the higher of the product rate plus 2% or a notional 5.5%. If rates have risen significantly since you first took the mortgage, your property may not pass the stress test at its current loan balance, forcing you to repay capital or accept a lower loan amount.

Should I fix or track my buy-to-let mortgage when rates are expected to fall?

A tracker will benefit you if rates fall as expected, passing the reduction through immediately. A fix protects you if rates stay elevated or rise further. The decision should reflect your cash flow tolerance and your view on the rate cycle — most landlords with multiple properties use a mix of both to spread the risk. Speaking to a whole-of-market broker will help you model both scenarios against your specific portfolio.
