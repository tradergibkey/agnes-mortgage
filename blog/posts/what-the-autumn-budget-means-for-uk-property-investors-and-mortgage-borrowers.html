What the Autumn Budget Means for UK Property Investors and Mortgage Borrowers

October 6, 2026 · UK Mortgage Market Insights & Rate Trends · Agnes Mortgage

Agnes Siry Licensed Mortgage Advisor October 6, 2026

The Chancellor's Autumn Budget delivered the largest set of tax changes affecting UK property in over a decade — and most investors are still working out what it actually costs them. By the end of this article you will understand exactly which measures hit landlords, company directors and mortgage borrowers hardest, and what actions are worth taking before the changes fully bed in.

What the Autumn Budget Actually Changed for Property

The Autumn Budget is the government's annual statement setting tax rates, spending plans and fiscal rules for the year ahead. For property, it is the single most consequential financial event because it governs stamp duty land tax (SDLT), capital gains tax (CGT), mortgage interest relief, and the macro conditions that drive Bank of England base rate decisions — and therefore your mortgage costs.

Why This Budget Matters More Than Most

Recent Budgets have been, in practice, holding exercises — politically cautious and fiscally constrained. This one was different. The government faced a stated £22 billion fiscal gap and chose to close a significant portion of it through property-related taxation. Three changes stand out:

  • Stamp Duty Land Tax surcharge on second homes rose from 3% to 5%, effective from the day after the Budget announcement.
  • Capital Gains Tax on residential property remained unchanged at 18% (basic rate) and 24% (higher rate) — but CGT on other assets increased, narrowing the relative advantage of holding property versus other investments.
  • Employer National Insurance Contributions rose to 15%, with the threshold lowered to £5,000 — a change that directly affects limited-company directors who pay themselves through a combination of salary and dividends, and who use that income to service a mortgage.

Each of these interacts with the mortgage market in ways that are not always obvious. The sections below unpack the mechanics.

The SDLT Surcharge Increase: Mechanics and Real Costs

The additional dwelling surcharge (ADS) — the extra SDLT rate applied to purchases of second homes, buy-to-let properties and company-owned residential assets — moved from 3% to 5% with no transition period. If you exchanged contracts before the Budget day but had not completed, the higher rate applied on completion.

On a £350,000 buy-to-let purchase, the SDLT bill under the old regime was approximately £8,000. Under the new 5% surcharge, it is approximately £15,500 — a £7,500 increase on a single transaction. On a £600,000 HMO, the difference is larger still.

For portfolio landlords assembling multiple units, this is a structural shift in acquisition economics. Gross rental yields need to be higher to justify the same entry price, or purchase prices need to fall. In practice, many investors are now negotiating harder on price, particularly in markets where vendor expectations have not yet adjusted.

One important nuance: the surcharge applies to corporate purchasers as well as individuals. Buying through a limited company does not exempt you from the 5% surcharge — it simply changes the tax treatment of rental income and eventual disposal. If you are weighing personal versus corporate ownership, that decision needs to be modelled on its full merits, not on a mistaken belief that companies escape the surcharge.

Capital Gains Tax: What Stayed, What Shifted, and What the Gap Now Looks Like

The Chancellor did not raise CGT on residential property disposals. The 18% and 24% rates for basic and higher-rate taxpayers remain. However, CGT on other assets — shares, business assets above the lifetime limit — moved up. This matters because the relative attractiveness of property as a long-term capital vehicle compared to equities has subtly changed.

For existing landlords deciding whether to hold or sell, the residential CGT rates are now actually lower relative to other asset classes than they were before the Budget. That is an argument for holding. But it has to be weighed against the direction of travel: a government that raised CGT on other assets once is more likely than not to revisit residential rates in future fiscal statements. Timing a disposal is always speculative, but the risk of a future CGT increase on residential property is now higher than it was twelve months ago.

Annual CGT exemptions remain at the reduced £3,000 level introduced in the previous fiscal year. For a landlord selling a property with £150,000 of embedded gain, the effective CGT bill at the higher rate is approximately £35,000 after the exemption. That number has not changed post-Budget — but it is no longer cushioned by a larger annual allowance or a lower rate expectation.

The NIC Changes and Their Effect on Director Borrowers

Limited-company directors who draw a modest salary — typically around the secondary threshold — and take the remainder of their remuneration as dividends will face higher employer NIC costs from April 2025. For directors who employ themselves through their own company, the practical effect is a reduction in the net income available to service a mortgage.

Most mortgage lenders assessing limited-company director income use one of two approaches: salary plus dividends drawn, or salary plus share of net profit. Neither approach automatically adjusts for higher NIC costs. You will need to model what your 2024–25 and 2025–26 accounts look like after the NIC changes before approaching a lender, particularly if you are targeting a high-income multiple or a large loan.

Base Rate Trajectory and What It Means for Mortgage Rates

The Budget's fiscal projections matter beyond the headline tax changes because they signal the path of inflation — and inflation is what the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee targets when setting base rate. The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) revised inflation forecasts upward following the Budget, partly because increased government spending adds demand-side pressure to the economy.

The consequence is that the Bank of England's rate-cutting cycle is likely to be shallower and slower than markets anticipated before the Budget. Swap rates — the underlying benchmark that lenders use to price fixed-rate mortgages — rose in the days immediately after the Budget announcement, and several lenders repriced their fixed-rate products upward within the following fortnight.

What does this mean practically? If you were planning to remortgage in the next six to twelve months on the assumption that rates would fall significantly, the Budget has introduced material uncertainty into that assumption. A two-year fix taken now may not look significantly worse than a product taken in twelve months' time, once arrangement fees and the risk of SVR exposure are factored in. The old consensus — "wait it out, rates will fall to 3.5% quickly" — is no longer well-supported by the data.

Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

Several misreadings of the Budget are circulating among investors. The most consequential are:

  • Assuming limited companies are SDLT-exempt. They are not. The 5% surcharge applies regardless of ownership vehicle.
  • Conflating CGT rate stability with CGT safety. Rates did not rise this Budget, but the annual exemption is already at a historic low and the political direction is clear.
  • Over-estimating how quickly base rate will fall. The OBR's revised inflation forecast makes a swift return to sub-4% mortgage rates unlikely in the near term.
  • Ignoring the NIC impact on director income assessments. A higher NIC burden lowers net extractable income — that ripples directly into affordability calculations.
  • Treating the Budget as a one-off event. The fiscal measures announced are scheduled to land in phases through 2025 and 2026. The full cost to a property portfolio compounds over time.

A Worked Example: Portfolio Landlord Re-evaluating a New Acquisition

Consider a higher-rate taxpayer who owns six buy-to-let properties in personal name and is assessing a seventh — a terraced house in the North West at an asking price of £220,000, expected rental income of £1,050 per month.

Pre-Budget SDLT cost: approximately £7,500 (including the old 3% surcharge).
Post-Budget SDLT cost: approximately £11,500 (including the new 5% surcharge).
Additional upfront cost: £4,000.

At a gross rental yield of 5.7%, the property generates £12,600 per year before costs and finance. The additional £4,000 SDLT cost represents approximately 31% of one year's gross rent — a meaningful drag on the payback period for the acquisition costs.

The landlord's mortgage adviser re-runs the numbers using an interest-only buy-to-let mortgage at 4.85% on a 75% LTV (£165,000 loan). Annual interest: approximately £8,003. After insurance, maintenance allowance and letting agent fees, the net annual return before tax is slim.

The correct response here is not necessarily to walk away from the deal. It is to negotiate the purchase price down by £8,000–£12,000 to restore the yield, or to identify whether the property qualifies for a higher-value HMO strategy that increases the rental income and justifies the entry cost. Neither option is visible without first running the post-Budget numbers correctly.

Key Takeaways

  • The SDLT additional dwelling surcharge rose from 3% to 5% immediately — on a £350,000 purchase that is roughly £7,500 more upfront, and it applies to both personal and corporate buyers.
  • CGT on residential property did not rise, but CGT on other assets did — and the annual exemption remains at a historic low of £3,000, so your effective tax on a disposal is still substantial.
  • The NIC increase will reduce net extractable income for limited-company directors from April 2025, affecting how lenders assess affordability for this borrower type.
  • Base rate is likely to fall more slowly than markets expected before the Budget; swap rate movements have already pushed some fixed-rate mortgage products upward.
  • The most dangerous response to this Budget is inaction based on misreadings — particularly the belief that limited companies escape the surcharge, or that rates will fall quickly enough to justify waiting.

If you are making acquisition, disposal or refinancing decisions in the next twelve months, the single most productive step is a structured review of your numbers before you approach a lender or make an offer. Get the income picture right, the SDLT modelled correctly, and the rate scenario stress-tested — in that order.

Frequently asked questions

Did the Autumn Budget raise stamp duty on buy-to-let properties?

Yes. The additional dwelling surcharge — the extra stamp duty applied to second homes and buy-to-let purchases — rose from 3% to 5% with immediate effect from the day after the Budget. On a £300,000 investment property, this adds approximately £6,000 to the upfront purchase cost.

Did Capital Gains Tax on property increase in the Autumn Budget?

No. CGT rates on residential property were left unchanged at 18% for basic-rate taxpayers and 24% for higher-rate taxpayers. However, CGT on other assets such as shares did increase, and the annual CGT exemption remains at the reduced level of £3,000 — so the effective tax on a disposal is still significant.

How does the Autumn Budget affect mortgage rates in the UK?

The Budget's increased spending projections led the Office for Budget Responsibility to revise inflation forecasts upward, which in turn reduced the likelihood of rapid Bank of England base rate cuts. Swap rates rose shortly after the announcement, and several lenders repriced fixed-rate mortgage products higher in the following weeks.

Does buying property through a limited company avoid the higher SDLT surcharge?

No. The 5% additional dwelling surcharge applies to limited-company purchases of residential property just as it does to personal buyers. A limited company structure changes how rental income and profits are taxed, but it does not exempt the purchase from the higher stamp duty rate.

How do the new National Insurance Contribution changes affect limited-company directors applying for a mortgage?

From April 2025, employer NIC rises to 15% with a lower threshold of £5,000. For directors who pay themselves a salary through their own company, this reduces the net income extractable from the business. Lenders assessing director income for mortgage affordability will see lower net figures on 2025–26 accounts, which can reduce the loan size available.
